The Matter

This page documents a disputed succession to a secure tenancy under the Housing Act 1985. The tenant, my father, died on 31 May 2026. I am his son and have occupied the property as my only or principal home since 1995.

Under section 89 of the Housing Act 1985, where a secure tenant dies and a person is qualified to succeed under section 87, the tenancy vests in that person by operation of law. The statutory language is mandatory. The landlord has no discretion to withhold succession where the conditions are met.

Peabody's position is that insufficient evidence of residence has been provided. I do not accept that position. The evidence is set out in the Evidence section. The legal framework is set out in the Legal Position section.

Current Status

Legal Position

1. Statutory Right to Succeed

The tenancy is a secure tenancy under the Housing Act 1985. It was created in approximately 1982 and assigned to my father in 1995 by License to Assign following a flat swap. Peabody's Notice of Seeking Possession confirms the tenancy type as secure.

Section 87 provides that a person is qualified to succeed if he occupies the dwelling-house as his only or principal home at the time of the tenant's death and is a member of the tenant's family who has resided with the tenant throughout the twelve months ending with the death.

I am the son of the tenant and have resided at the property throughout that period.

Section 89 provides that where there is a person qualified to succeed under section 87, the tenancy "vests by virtue of this section" in that person. The tenancy therefore passes automatically by operation of law. It is not a decision the landlord can grant or refuse.

The statutory right applies only once. My father obtained the tenancy by assignment in 1995, not by succession. The previous tenant's tenancy agreement dated 1986 is on file.

2. Procedural Defects in the Notice to Quit

Service on the Public Trustee

Section 18 of the Law of Property (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1994 requires that a notice to quit served after a tenant's death be (a) addressed to the Personal Representatives at the deceased's last known place of residence and (b) a copy served on the Public Trustee.

The Notice to Quit dated 16 September 2026 is addressed to the Personal Representatives of Mr Nazir Osman. There is no indication on its face that a copy was served on the Public Trustee.

Gateway Housing Association v Ali [2020] EWCA Civ 1339

The Court of Appeal considered section 18(1)(b) in Gateway Housing Association v Personal Representatives of Ali, deceased [2020] EWCA Civ 1339. The court held that the notice must be clear to any person who may legitimately have an interest in its validity. A copy served on the Public Trustee must be received by the date the notice is expressed to take effect.

If no copy was served on the Public Trustee, or it was served out of time, the notice is likely invalid and can be challenged as a defence to any possession claim.

Service on a Deceased Person

The notice is addressed to the Personal Representatives, which is correct in form. However, no grant of representation has been obtained. The deceased's property vests in the Public Trustee until representation is granted. The notice is therefore valid only if the copy was served on the Public Trustee as required.

3. Key Precedents

Clarion Housing Association Ltd v Carter [2021] EWHC 2890 (QB)

Ms Carter moved into her mother's property in 2004 as her carer. Clarion refused succession, served a Notice to Quit, and brought possession proceedings. The High Court dismissed Clarion's appeal. The court found that Ms Carter satisfied the succession clause in the tenancy agreement, that the service of the Notice to Quit was not proved effective, and that the landlord's decision was "tainted by illegality and procedural unfairness".

This establishes that a family member who lived with and cared for the deceased tenant can succeed, and that procedural defects in the Notice to Quit can invalidate the landlord's claim.

Peabody Trust (202320788) — Housing Ombudsman

The Ombudsman found maladministration in Peabody's management of a secure tenancy succession. Peabody delayed unreasonably, issued Notices to Quit, and incorrectly told the resident she would not statutorily succeed. Peabody's own records showed it had accepted the succession on 27 October 2022, yet it wrote to the resident on 31 October 2022 saying she did not qualify. The Ombudsman ordered an apology, £300 compensation, and staff training on succession rights across all tenures.

Peabody Trust (202202211) — Housing Ombudsman

The Ombudsman found severe maladministration where Peabody failed to respond to a resident's succession-related complaints for over a year. The Ombudsman found multiple failures and ordered an apology and £1,200 compensation.

Governors of the Peabody Trust v Lawrence

A daughter who had lived with her mother since childhood and was her carer successfully defended a possession claim. The court found it was "not reasonable" to grant possession, as the risk of harm to the resident if moved outweighed the benefit of freeing up the property.

Regulator of Social Housing — C2 Grading

In April 2026 the Regulator of Social Housing issued Peabody a C2 Consumer grading, meaning "there are some weaknesses in it delivering the outcomes of the consumer standards". Areas for improvement include repairs, complaints handling, data quality, and resident engagement.

The data quality and complaints handling weaknesses are directly relevant: Peabody's fraud check produced a result contradicted by the Ealing Council vote-by-post letter, and Peabody's correspondence has been contradictory and delayed.

Housing Ombudsman Performance Data

Peabody's complaints report for 2025 records 231 determinations, a 70% maladministration rate, and 40 findings of severe maladministration. The Ombudsman's special report on Catalyst Housing (pre-merger with Peabody) found the landlord "routinely failed to recognise formal complaints" and described its service as "arbitrary, disjointed and concerning".

This is a documented, systemic pattern rather than an isolated failure.
