Legal Position

Purpose

This document sets out the legal framework applicable to my succession case, the statutory basis for my right to succeed, and the procedural defects in Peabody's Notice to Quit.

1. Statutory Right to Succeed

The Governing Legislation

My father's tenancy is a secure tenancy under the Housing Act 1985. The tenancy was created in approximately 1982 (the previous tenant's agreement dated 1986 is on file) and was assigned to my father in 1995 via a License to Assign following a flat swap. Peabody's Notice of Seeking Possession confirms the tenancy type as secure.

Section 87 — Persons Qualified to Succeed

Under Section 87 of the Housing Act 1985, a person is qualified to succeed the tenant under a secure tenancy if:

"he occupies the dwelling-house as his only or principal home at the time of the tenant's death and either— (a) he is the tenant's spouse or civil partner, or (b) he is another member of the tenant's family and has resided with the tenant throughout the period of twelve months ending with the tenant's death." [citation:15]

I am the son of the tenant and therefore "another member of the tenant's family." I have resided at the property throughout the 12 months ending with my father's death on 31 May 2026.

Section 89 — Succession to Periodic Tenancy

Under Section 89 of the Housing Act 1985, where a secure tenant dies and the tenancy is a periodic tenancy:

"Where there is a person qualified to succeed the tenant under section 87, the tenancy vests by virtue of this section in that person." [citation:11]

The word "vests" is critical. It means the tenancy passes to me automatically by operation of law. It is not a decision that Peabody can grant or refuse at their discretion.

The "One Succession" Rule

The statutory right of succession applies only once. I am satisfied that there has been no previous succession. My father obtained the tenancy via a License to Assign in 1995, following a mutual exchange with the previous tenant. This was an assignment, not a succession. The previous tenant's tenancy agreement from 1986 is held on file as evidence.

2. Procedural Defects in the Notice to Quit

Service on the Public Trustee

Under Section 18 of the Law of Property (Miscellaneous Provisions) Act 1994, a notice to quit served after a tenant's death must be:

"(a) addressed to 'The Personal Representatives of' the deceased (naming him) and left at or sent by post to his last known place of residence or business in the United Kingdom, and
(b) a copy of it, similarly addressed, is served on the Public Trustee." [citation:19]

The Notice to Quit dated 16 September 2026 is addressed to "The Personal Representatives of Mr Nazir Osman." However, there is no indication on the face of the notice that a copy was served on the Public Trustee.

Gateway Housing Association v Ali [2020] EWCA Civ 1339

The Court of Appeal in Gateway Housing Association v Personal Representatives of Ali, deceased [2020] EWCA Civ 1339 considered the requirements of Section 18(1)(b) in detail [citation:26]. The court held that:

"the notice to quit needs to be clear to any person who may legitimately have an interest in the validity of the notice" [citation:26].

In that case, Gateway served the notice on the PRs at the property on 17 October 2018, and sent a copy to the Public Trustee on 18 October 2018, which was received on 30 October 2018. The Court of Appeal held that the notice took effect on 18 November 2018, four weeks after service on the PRs at the property, and that the copy served on the Public Trustee did not affect this conclusion, provided it was received by the date the notice was expressed to take effect [citation:39].

Application to my case: If Peabody has not served a copy of the Notice to Quit on the Public Trustee at all, or served it after the expiry date, the notice is likely invalid. This is a procedural defect that can be raised as a defence to any possession claim.

Service on a Deceased Person

The notice is addressed to "The Personal Representatives of Mr Nazir Osman." I am the son of the deceased and the person seeking to succeed to the tenancy. However, no grant of representation has been obtained. The property of the deceased vests in the Public Trustee until representation is granted [citation:32]. Peabody's notice to the PRs is procedurally correct in form, but only valid if the copy was served on the Public Trustee as required.

3. Key Legal Precedents

Clarion Housing Association Ltd v Carter [2021] EWHC 2890 (QB)

This is the leading case on succession to a secure tenancy and the service of notices after a tenant's death.

Facts: Ms Carter moved into her mother's property in 2004 to act as her carer. Her mother died in June 2017. Clarion refused her succession, served a Notice to Quit, and brought possession proceedings. Clarion argued that Ms Carter had no right to succeed because the property was not her principal home and because she was a stranger to the tenancy contract.

Decision: The High Court dismissed Clarion's appeal. The court found:

  • Ms Carter satisfied the requirements of the succession clause in the tenancy agreement. She had lived at the property as her main home and sought to have the tenancy vested in her within the required time.
  • The judge's decision that she satisfied the six-month requirement was "unassailable and correct" [citation:4].
  • The service of the Notice to Quit was not proved to be effective. The Public Trustee's copy was not served before the expiry of the notice [citation:14].

Relevance to my case: This case establishes that a family member who lived with and cared for the deceased tenant can succeed to the tenancy, and that procedural defects in the Notice to Quit can invalidate the landlord's claim. It also establishes that the landlord's decision to refuse succession may be "tainted by illegality and procedural unfairness" if the landlord fails to apply its own policy correctly.

Peabody Trust (202320788) — Housing Ombudsman

The Ombudsman found maladministration in Peabody's management of a secure tenancy succession request [citation:21].

Facts: The resident's mother was the sole secure tenant. The resident claimed succession. Peabody delayed over two years, issued Notices to Quit, and incorrectly told the resident she would not statutorily succeed. Peabody's internal records showed it had accepted the succession on 27 October 2022, but it wrote to the resident on 31 October 2022 saying she did not qualify.

Findings: The Ombudsman found:

  • Peabody "delayed unreasonably" in dealing with the succession claim.
  • Peabody's position "did not comply with its policy statement" and was "outside the rights enshrined in law" [citation:21].
  • Peabody had a "lack of understanding of the resident's legal position" [citation:21].

Orders: Peabody was ordered to apologise in writing, pay £300 compensation, and provide staff training to ensure relevant staff understand succession rights of all tenures [citation:21].

Relevance to my case: This is directly relevant. Peabody has a documented history of mismanaging secure tenancy successions, delaying unreasonably, and failing to understand the legal position. This supports my case that their refusal of my succession is procedurally flawed.

Peabody Trust (202202211) — Housing Ombudsman

The Ombudsman found severe maladministration where Peabody failed to respond to a resident's succession-related complaints and left him without contact for over a year [citation:22].

Findings: The Ombudsman found "multiple failures in the landlord's handling of this issue" and noted that the resident had lost all faith in the landlord [citation:22].

Orders: Peabody was ordered to apologise and pay £1,200 compensation [citation:22].

Relevance to my case: This demonstrates a pattern of Peabody failing to engage with residents in succession matters and causing significant distress through delay and non-communication.

Governors of the Peabody Trust v Lawrence

In this case, a daughter who had lived with her mother since childhood and was her carer successfully defended a possession claim.

Decision: The court found it was "not reasonable" to grant possession. The risk of harm to the resident if moved outweighed the social benefit of freeing up the property.

Relevance to my case: This case supports my position that a court will consider the personal circumstances and caring role of a family member when deciding whether it is reasonable to evict. My 30-year residence and role as my father's carer are directly relevant.
